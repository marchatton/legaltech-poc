# Approach Landscape Template (April Dunford style, respectful)

Use this template to write a short, credible “competitors” section without making it a vendor shootout.
This is an **approach landscape**: alternatives (including status quo) + constraints + why our approach is differentiated.

## 1) Start with positioning (10 to 15 seconds)
Say:
- “Before I show more product, here’s the context: this is a {{category_or_context}}.”
- “There are a few sensible ways to solve this. Most teams choose an approach based on what they optimise for.”

## 2) Alternatives + discovery (30 to 45 seconds)
Ask (pick 1 to 2, keep it conversational):
- “What are you doing today to {{job_to_be_done}}?”
- “What have you tried already, and what broke down?”
- “What constraints matter most: accuracy/provenance, speed, compliance, change management, or cost?”
- “If nothing changed, what happens? What does ‘no decision’ look like?”

Then describe common market approaches (neutral). Choose one or two patterns. Keep neutral language.

Pattern A: Speed and breadth
- What it looks like: fast responses, broad coverage, lighter grounding
- Why teams do it: optimises for adoption and quick time-to-value
- Trade-off: more variance in reliability and provenance

Pattern B: Workflow automation
- What it looks like: strict flows, deterministic steps, limited flexibility
- Why teams do it: optimises for repeatable processes and compliance
- Trade-off: less adaptable to messy questions and edge cases

Pattern C: Deep retrieval and provenance
- What it looks like: stronger grounding, citations, controlled outputs
- Why teams do it: optimises for trust and high-stakes usage
- Trade-off: more complexity and sometimes more latency

## 3) Map competitors to approaches (optional, only if names provided)
If competitor names are provided, map them to patterns without judgement and without dunking:
- “{{competitor_1}} tends to lean towards {{pattern}} because it optimises for {{priority}}.”
- “{{competitor_2}} leans towards {{pattern}}.”

If names are not provided, keep it generic:
- “Many teams lean towards {{pattern}} when {{constraint}} is the priority.”

## 4) Define “perfect world” (10 to 15 seconds)
Say:
- “In a perfect world for {{segment.end_user}}, ‘good’ looks like: {{perfect_world_criteria_1}}, {{perfect_world_criteria_2}}, {{perfect_world_criteria_3}}.”

## 5) Our differentiated value (tie to user + constraints)
Say:
- “We’re optimising for {{our_priority}}, because our user is trying to {{job_to_be_done}} while feeling {{starting_feelings}}.”
- “So we chose {{our_approach_summary}}.”
- “This is why you’d pick this approach vs the alternatives: {{differentiated_value_sentence}}.”

## 6) Proof (keep it buyer-relevant)
Say (pick one):
- “Proof: {{case_study_or_result}}.” (if true)
- “Evidence is preliminary: {{evidence_note}}.” (PoC-safe if you do not have proof yet)

## 7) Trade-offs (explicit and honest)
State:
- “Trade-off we accept: {{tradeoff_1}}.”
- “Trade-off we accept: {{tradeoff_2}}.”
- “Upside: {{upside}}.”

Close with:
- “It’s not ‘better’, it’s designed for different constraints.”

## 8) Objections + ask (optional, but recommended)
Objections (pick one you expect):
- “A fair concern is {{objection}}. Here’s how we handle it: {{response}}.”

Ask:
- “If this direction is useful, the next step is {{next_step}}.”

## Keep this out (Dunford don’ts)
Avoid:
- dunking
- a “firehose of features” walkthrough without context
- claims you cannot evidence
- investor-pitch content (TAM, destiny, disruption theatre)
- vague “AI magic” language
